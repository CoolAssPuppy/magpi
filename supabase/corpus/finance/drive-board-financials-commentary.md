## What I want the board to take away

We can fund the pilot line. We cannot fund the first production build from this
balance sheet. The gap between those two sentences is the entire finance
conversation for the next eight weeks, and it is a financing question rather
than an operating one.

Everything else in this commentary is the detail underneath those two
sentences.

## The number that matters

The first production build of 10,000 units is $11.4M of material and
conversion at the current unit cost. We hold $6.0M. Nothing about the
pilot line, the certification schedule or the launch plan changes that
arithmetic, and no amount of good execution between now and then produces the
money.

So the sequence is: pilot build proves the yield, the yield firms the unit
cost, and the firm unit cost is what we raise against. Any conversation that
tries to reorder those three steps is a conversation about hope.

## Unit economics

Unit cost at pilot volume closed at $1,140 on 2 September. The walkthrough is
in this space and I will not repeat it here beyond the four lines that move:
the display stack at $440, because that is what four folding panels cost, the
hinge assembly at $156 from a single supplier, assembly labour at $58 because
the pilot line puts the hinges in by hand, and a $19 lamination scrap allowance
because nobody has run this bond on four panels.

Jane set the launch price at $1,899 on 4 September. That holds roughly 40
percent gross margin before channel costs.

At 10,000 units, every dollar of unit cost is $10,000 of gross profit. The four
lines above are worth $673 of the $1,140 between them, and three of the four
improve with volume without anybody being clever. That is the case for
patience on margin and the reason I have not asked anyone to take cost out of
the product this quarter.

## The outer layer reversal, in money

The board will have read Jane's account. The finance version is short.

The material change cost us $18,000 in vendor engineering time on the work we
abandoned and saved us $53,000 on the lamination fixture, because the polymer
fixture is $214,000 against $267,000 for the glass one. Net $35,000 in our
favour on capex, with a further $19,000 of cosmetic re-measurement charged
against contingency. On unit cost the polymer material is $7 cheaper than the
glass and carries a $19 scrap allowance until the pilot build tells us the
truth, so today it is a wash.

The money is the smaller half of this. What is worth reporting is that the
decision moved to the Thursday rather than the following week, because I
flagged that the tooling commit was on the Monday. A material change after a
tooling commit means paying for a fixture we then throw away. Finance being in
the room early was worth the $53,000 difference between the two fixtures and
the six weeks we did not lose, and I would like the board to press on whether
that habit is holding, because it is four weeks old.

## What I am asking the board for

1. A view on timing for the raise. My own is that the conversation starts before
   the pilot build finishes and closes after it, because the yield number is the
   strongest thing we will have to show and waiting for it costs us six weeks.
2. Approval to commit the pilot build material in October, ahead of the yield
   result, because the lead times leave no other order of events.
3. An introduction to anybody the board knows who has financed a hardware
   company between a pilot line and a production build, which is the least
   fundable moment in the life of a device.
